\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Sampling several candidate solutions and letting a learned verifier pick one
(Best-of-$N$) is one of the simplest ways to turn extra inference compute into accuracy
on mathematical reasoning \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k,lightman2023verify,snell2024scaling}. The
verifier is usually a reward model of a few billion parameters: an outcome reward model
(ORM) that scores a complete response, or a process reward model (PRM) that scores each
reasoning step. In deployment it competes with the generator for accelerator memory. A
natural way to make room is post-training quantization (PTQ) of the verifier's weights
to 4 bits or fewer \cite{frantar2023gptq,lin2024awq,dettmers2023qlora}.

Whether this preserves selection quality is not obvious. PTQ is usually evaluated by
perplexity or by the accuracy of the quantized model on its own task, whereas a
verifier only needs to \emph{rank} candidates so that a correct one ends up on top.
Small score perturbations are harmless if they do not change which answer wins, but
Best-of-$N$ is sensitive to errors at the top of the ranking
\cite{gao2023overoptimization}. One might therefore expect quantization damage to grow
with the budget $N$ and with problem difficulty, and to matter most for small verifiers.
Conversely, if quantized verifiers select as well as full-precision ones, a memory
budget may be better spent on a larger quantized verifier.

We study these questions with frozen candidate pools and prospectively specified
analysis plans. A pool of 32 candidates per problem is generated once, so every verifier
and precision ranks exactly the same responses and differences in accuracy can only come
from the verifier. We evaluate three ORM sizes and one PRM in BF16, NF4, GPTQ (4 and 3
bits), AWQ and round-to-nearest on 2,000 GSM8K and MATH problems, measure verifier memory
with packed 4-bit kernels, and add two follow-up studies, with a stronger generator and
with new hard problems, whose plans were fixed before their candidates were generated.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Accuracy equivalence at 4 bits.} GPTQ-W4 meets a prespecified $\pm3$\pp\
equivalence bound for Best-of-32 accuracy of an 8B ORM (90\% interval $[-0.58, +1.25]$),
and the result holds in two follow-up studies for both an ORM and a PRM. Three-bit GPTQ
with generic calibration degrades selection, severely for the smallest verifier; in an
exploratory test, calibrating on in-domain candidates reduced the 1.7B loss from 13.2 to
3.8\pp.
\item \textbf{Retained verifier value.} In a follow-up study on 1,060 new hard problems,
where the verifiers beat majority vote, 4-bit quantization keeps more than half of the
advantage (prespecified test): the PRM retains an estimated 93\% of an 8.2\pp\
advantage, the ORM 91\% of a 3.0\pp\ advantage (narrowly significant). With a strong
generator, no clear verifier advantage over majority vote was established.
\item \textbf{A tested memory comparison.} Under a 6~GiB verifier-only budget and a fixed
workload, a 4B ORM with packed 4-bit weights outperforms the best tested full-precision
verifier that fits by 2.25\pp\ under the prespecified grader and 1.40\pp\ after a blinded
human audit of unparseable winners.
\item \textbf{Candidate-level instability without accuracy loss.} Quantization changes
the selected candidate on a quarter to a half of problems at 4 bits, but the selected
answer far less often; harm and benefit stay small and largely offset, consistent with
answer redundancy in the pools.
\item \textbf{Evaluation lessons.} A strict answer grader can turn reward-model format
preferences into spurious quantization gains. We show how a format/content
decomposition, a lenient grader, a blinded human audit and revised labels resolve this,
and we report two prespecified hypotheses (damage concentrated on MATH, growing with $N$)
that the evaluation data did not support.
\end{enumerate}
