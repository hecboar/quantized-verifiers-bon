\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Sampling several candidate solutions and letting a learned verifier pick one
(Best-of-$N$) is one of the simplest ways to turn extra inference compute into accuracy
on mathematical reasoning \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k,lightman2023verify,snell2024scaling}. The
verifier is usually a reward model of a few billion parameters: an outcome reward model
(ORM) that scores a complete response, or a process reward model (PRM) that scores each
reasoning step. In deployment it competes with the generator for accelerator memory. A
natural way to make room is post-training quantization (PTQ) of the verifier's weights
to 4 bits or fewer \cite{frantar2023gptq,lin2024awq,dettmers2023qlora}.

Whether this is safe is not obvious. PTQ is usually evaluated by perplexity or by the
accuracy of the quantized model on its own task, whereas a verifier only needs to
\emph{rank} candidates correctly. Small score perturbations are harmless if they do not
reorder the top of the ranking, but Best-of-$N$ amplifies errors at the top: as $N$
grows, the selected candidate is increasingly the one whose score error is largest
\cite{gao2023overoptimization}. One might therefore expect quantization damage to grow
with the budget $N$ and with problem difficulty, and to matter most for small
verifiers. Conversely, if quantized verifiers select as well as full-precision ones, a
memory budget is better spent on a larger quantized verifier than on a smaller
full-precision one.

We study these questions in a preregistered, controlled setting. A fixed pool of 32
candidates per problem is generated once and frozen, so that every verifier and every
precision ranks exactly the same responses and differences in accuracy can only come
from the verifier. We evaluate three ORM sizes and one PRM in BF16, NF4, GPTQ (4 and 3
bits), AWQ and round-to-nearest, on 2,000 GSM8K and MATH problems, and measure memory
with packed 4-bit kernels. Five contrasts were registered before any evaluation score
was opened; two follow-up studies with a stronger generator and with new problems were
registered before their data existed.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Equivalence at 4 bits.} Quantizing an 8B ORM with GPTQ-W4 leaves
Best-of-32 accuracy unchanged within $\pm1.3$\pp\ (registered equivalence test at
$\pm3$\pp), and the result replicates in two preregistered follow-up studies, with a 7B math
generator and with the original generator on new hard problems, for both an ORM and a
PRM. Three bits is where degradation
starts, sharply so for the smallest verifier.
\item \textbf{A memory-budget rule.} Under a 6~GB verifier budget, a 4B ORM with packed
4-bit weights beats the best full-precision verifier that fits by 2.25\pp\ (registered;
1.4\pp\ after correcting the strict grader with exhaustive blinded human grading).
\item \textbf{A mechanism.} Quantization changes the selected candidate on a quarter to
a third of problems at 4 bits, but rarely the correctness of the selected answer,
because candidates are redundant: damage is confined to contested problems where
competing answers have similar support, and quantization noise tends to move the
selection towards the consensus answer.
\item \textbf{Value retention.} Where the verifier beats majority vote, 4-bit
quantization keeps most of that advantage: in a preregistered study on 1,060 new hard
problems the PRM retains 93\% of an 8.2\pp\ advantage (the ORM 91\% of 3.0\pp, narrowly
significant). With a strong generator, however, majority vote matches or beats
reward-model selection on hard problems, which limits how much value a verifier can add
in the first place.
\item \textbf{Evaluation lessons.} Strict answer graders can turn reward-model format
preferences into spurious quantization ``gains''; we show how a format/content
decomposition, a lenient grader, a blinded human audit and revised labels resolve this, and
report two registered hypotheses (damage grows with difficulty and with $N$) that the
evaluation data did not support.
\end{enumerate}
