\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Deployment implications} For the tested reward-model verifiers of 4B
parameters and above, 4-bit weight-only GPTQ preserved Best-of-$N$ accuracy in three
prospectively planned settings (two generators, three problem sets, an ORM family and
a PRM), and where the verifier beat majority vote it kept most of that advantage. NF4
and AWQ behaved similarly in the exploratory grid but were not part of the follow-up
studies. Two reward models on a Llama backbone also stayed within the margin, but lost
0.6--1.5\pp\ and changed their selections more often, so the size of the 4-bit effect
varies between models even when it is small. Under the tested 6~GB verifier budget and workload, a 4B ORM with packed
4-bit weights was the best tested deployment, ahead of the best full-precision
verifier that fit. Three bits was not robust with generic calibration: it cost
1.3--1.8\pp\ for the larger verifiers in the main study, 2.6--3.5\pp\ in H7, and
13.2\pp\ for the 1.7B ORM, which was also sensitive to the quantization method at 4 bits
(RTN versus GPTQ), and 12.6--26.4\pp\ for the two Llama-based reward models. Calibrating on in-domain candidates instead of generic text removed
most of this 3-bit loss in an exploratory test (8B ORM: $-0.05$\pp; 1.7B: $-3.75$
instead of $-13.15$\pp), so the calibration data matter as much as the bit width at
3 bits. These conclusions are specific to the tested checkpoints, quantization recipes,
scoring rule and mathematical tasks.

\paragraph{Why accuracy is stable when selections change} Quantization perturbs
verifier scores enough to change the selected candidate on a quarter to a half of
problems at 4 bits, yet the selected canonical answer changes far less often
(Table~\ref{tab:transitions}): most changes stay within the same answer cluster, and
the remaining harms and benefits largely offset each other. This is consistent with
answer redundancy in the pools: Best-of-$N$ needs the top of the ranking to keep a
correct answer, not a particular candidate. We have not isolated this mechanism
causally (for example by manipulating answer multiplicities), and the remaining
answer-level changes are concentrated where competing answers have similar support. It
suggests that quantization sensitivity should depend on how often answers compete
closely rather than on nominal difficulty or on $N$, which agrees with the unsupported
difficulty and budget contrasts (c4, c5).

\paragraph{How much a verifier is worth} Asking whether quantization destroys the
value a verifier adds presupposes that the verifier adds value. With the main
generator the Skywork ORMs were within about one point of majority vote overall; with a
strong math generator on hard problems majority vote matched the PRM and beat the ORM;
only in the regime selected for verifier advantage (H7) did both verifiers clearly beat
majority vote. Aggregation rules that combine answer frequency and reward scores can
change this comparison \cite{kuang2025aggregation}; our weighted-vote control shows the
same robustness to quantization under such a rule. Practitioners should compare a
verifier against self-consistency on their own generator before paying its memory and
latency cost.

\paragraph{Evaluating selection} Three practices changed our conclusions or their size.
(i) A strict answer grader turned a reward-model preference for prose answers into
apparent quantization ``gains'' of up to 8\pp; a format/content decomposition and a
lenient grader removed them, and a blinded human audit of the unparseable winners
reduced the registered memory-budget effect from 2.25 to 1.4\pp. (ii) A fixed-precision
null control (same weights, different batch layout) changes 5--15\% of selected
candidates across our studies; winner-change rates without such a reference overstate
instability. (iii) Development-set signals that motivated two registered hypotheses did
not replicate on 1,200 evaluation problems; registering them made the non-replication
visible.

\paragraph{Limitations} The main-study ORMs come from a single family (Skywork-Reward-V2,
built on Qwen3), the PRM is a Qwen model and the generators are Qwen and Llama models;
the two additional Llama-based reward models address the backbone and training-family
question only in an exploratory way, and neither added value over majority vote, so
value retention outside the Qwen-based verifiers is untested. Accuracy experiments use simulated quantization; packed 4-bit
kernels agreed with it on 98.8--99.2\% of selected answers, and memory was measured for
one scoring workload without the generator. The confirmatory GPTQ results use generic calibration with a
single calibration draw; in-domain calibration was tested only at 3 bits and for two
verifiers. The tasks are GSM8K and MATH, whose test problems may have been
seen during pre-training of the generators and verifiers; because BF16 and quantized
verifiers score the same frozen pools, exposure is shared by design, but it may alter
pool redundancy and verifier advantage and could interact with quantization.
Candidates were capped at 1,024 or 2,048 tokens and 6.5--19\% reached the cap, although
excluding them changes no conclusion. The human audit was performed by a single rater
on the unparseable winners only, and its corrected estimate treats the labels as
error-free. The value-retention result is confirmatory for the PRM and only narrowly
so for the ORM, and it holds in a regime chosen with prior knowledge; we did not find a
strong-generator regime in which the verifiers clearly beat majority vote. Finally, the
analysis plans were fixed in version control without an external registry, and the
main-study plan was amended after candidate generation but before any verifier
scoring.
