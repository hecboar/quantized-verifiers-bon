\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Deployment implications} For the tested reward-model verifiers with 4B or
more parameters, 4-bit weight-only GPTQ preserved Best-of-$N$ accuracy in three
prospectively planned settings, which covered two generators, three problem sets, an
ORM family and a PRM. Where the verifier beat majority vote, the quantized verifier kept
most of that advantage. NF4 and AWQ behaved similarly in the exploratory grid but were
not part of the follow-up studies. Two reward models on a Llama backbone also stayed
within the margin. They lost 0.6--1.5\pp\ and changed their selections more often,
though, so the size of the 4-bit effect varies between models even when it is small.
Under the tested 6~GiB verifier budget and workload, a 4B ORM with packed 4-bit weights
was the best tested deployment, ahead of the best full-precision verifier that fit.

Three-bit GPTQ with generic calibration was not robust. It cost 1.3--1.8\pp\ for the
larger verifiers in the main study, 2.6--3.5\pp\ in H7, 12.6--26.4\pp\ for the two
Llama-based reward models and 13.2\pp\ for the 1.7B ORM, which was also sensitive to the
choice between RTN and GPTQ at 4 bits. In exploratory tests, calibrating on in-domain
candidates instead of generic text substantially reduced this loss for both models that
had failed, from $-13.15$ to $-3.75$\pp\ for the 1.7B ORM and from $-12.60$ to
$-2.80$\pp\ for Tulu-3. The 8B ORM and the PRM, which lost little to begin with, moved
to $-0.05$ and $-0.60$\pp. A length-matched generic control did not help, so the
content of the calibration text matters, although we cannot say whether its domain or
its format is responsible. At 3 bits, the calibration recipe matters as much as the bit
width. All of
these conclusions are specific to the tested checkpoints, quantization recipes, scoring
rule and mathematical tasks.

\paragraph{Why accuracy is stable when selections change} At 4 bits quantization
perturbs verifier scores enough to change the selected candidate on a quarter to a half
of problems, yet the selected canonical answer changes far less often
(Table~\ref{tab:transitions}). Most changes stay within the same answer cluster, and the
remaining harms and benefits largely cancel. This is consistent with answer redundancy
in the pools. For Best-of-$N$ to stay correct, the top of the ranking has to keep a
correct answer, and any of the candidates that carry it will do. We have not isolated
this mechanism causally, for example by manipulating answer multiplicities, and we have
not measured where the remaining answer-level changes occur. A testable implication is
that quantization sensitivity depends on how closely answers compete within a pool more
than on nominal difficulty or on $N$.

\paragraph{How much a verifier is worth} Testing whether quantization preserves a
verifier's advantage over majority vote requires a setting where the full-precision
verifier has one. With the main generator, the BF16 Skywork ORMs were within $-0.3$ to
$+1.4$\pp\ of majority vote. With the strong math generator on hard problems (H6),
majority vote matched the PRM and beat the 8B ORM. Both verifiers clearly beat majority
vote only in H7, the regime we had selected for verifier advantage. Aggregation rules
that combine answer frequency and reward scores can change this comparison
\cite{kuang2025aggregation}, and our weighted-vote control shows the same robustness to
quantization under such a rule. Practitioners should compare a verifier with
self-consistency on their own generator before paying its memory and latency cost.

\paragraph{Evaluating selection} Three practices changed our conclusions or the size of
our estimates. First, a strict answer grader turned a reward-model preference for prose
answers into apparent quantization ``gains'' of up to 8\pp. A format/content
decomposition and a lenient grader substantially reduced these gains, and a blinded
human audit of the winners without an extractable answer reduced the prespecified
memory-budget effect from 2.25 to 1.4\pp. Second, a fixed-precision null control (same
weights, different batch layout) changes 5--15\% of selected candidates across our
studies, so winner-change rates without such a reference overstate instability. Third,
two development-set signals that motivated prespecified hypotheses did not replicate on
1,200 evaluation problems, and prespecifying them made the non-replication visible.

\paragraph{Limitations} The main-study ORMs come from a single family
(Skywork-Reward-V2 on Qwen3), the PRM is also Qwen-based, and the generators are Qwen and
Llama models. The two additional Llama-based reward models vary the backbone and the
training family only in exploratory runs. Neither beat majority vote in BF16, so value
retention is untested outside the Qwen-based verifiers. Accuracy experiments use
simulated quantization. Packed 4-bit kernels agreed with it on 98.8--99.2\% of selected
answers, and memory and throughput were measured for one scoring workload without the
generator. The confirmatory GPTQ results use generic calibration with a single
calibration draw, and in-domain calibration was tested only at 3 bits, for four
verifiers and with one calibration draw. The tasks are GSM8K and MATH, whose test problems may have been seen during
pre-training of the generators and verifiers. Because BF16 and quantized verifiers
score the same frozen pools, any exposure is shared by design, but it may alter pool
redundancy and verifier advantage and could interact with quantization. Candidates were
capped at 1,024 or 2,048 tokens and 6.5--19\% reached the cap, although excluding them
changes no conclusion. The human audit was done by a single rater on all winners without
an extractable answer plus 30 controls with one, and its corrected estimate treats the
labels as error-free. The value-retention result is confirmatory for the PRM and only
narrowly so for the ORM, and it holds in a regime chosen with prior knowledge. We did
not find a strong-generator regime in which the verifiers clearly beat majority vote.
Finally, the analysis plans were fixed in version control without an external registry,
and the main-study plan was amended after candidate generation but before any verifier
scoring.
