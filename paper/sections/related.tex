\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Verifiers and test-time compute.} Training a verifier to rank sampled
solutions was introduced for grade-school mathematics by \citet{cobbe2021gsm8k}. Process
supervision, which scores every reasoning step, outperforms outcome supervision on MATH
\cite{lightman2023verify}, and automatically labelled PRMs
\cite{wang2023mathshepherd,setlur2024rewarding,zhang2025prm} and generative verifiers
\cite{zhang2024generative} have followed. How to spend inference compute between
sampling and verification has become a research question in itself
\cite{snell2024scaling,liu2025can,singhi2025when}. These works study what verifiers can
achieve at full precision; the cost of the verifier itself enters only as a fixed term.

\paragraph{Reward models against self-consistency.} Majority vote over sampled answers
(self-consistency) needs no verifier \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, and its coverage
and accuracy keep growing with the number of samples
\cite{brown2024large,wu2024inference}. Whether a reward model beats it depends on the
generator, the reward model and the aggregation rule
\cite{yuan2024free,di2025bestofmajority}. Most relevant to us, \citet{kim2025not}
compare memory-optimal test-time strategies for reasoning models and find that an
external 7B PRM, counted at full precision, is consistently less memory-efficient than
majority voting. We complement this result from the verifier side: quantizing the
verifier removes most of that memory cost without changing its selections, so the
comparison should be made against quantized verifiers.

\paragraph{Reward-model reliability.} Reward-model quality is usually measured on
preference and step-error benchmarks \cite{lambert2024rewardbench,zheng2024processbench,
song2025prmbench}, and Best-of-$N$ is known to over-optimise imperfect reward models
\cite{gao2023overoptimization}. Our question is different: not whether a reward model is
accurate, but whether a compressed copy of it makes the same selections as the original.

\paragraph{Post-training quantization of language models.} Post-training quantization
reduces memory with little loss of perplexity or task accuracy at 8 bits
\cite{dettmers2022llmint,xiao2022smoothquant} and, for weights only, at 4 bits
\cite{frantar2023gptq,lin2024awq,dettmers2023qlora},
4-bit precision is close to optimal for a fixed bit budget \cite{dettmers2022the}, and
large evaluations confirm that well-calibrated 4- and 8-bit models track their
full-precision versions \cite{kurtic2024give}; the topic is active in this journal as
well \cite{zhu2027rotsq,bang2027bvsr}. For reasoning, quantization of the
\emph{generator} hurts more at 3 bits and on harder problems
\cite{liu2025quantization,li2025quantization}. Aggregate accuracy can also hide many
changed answers: compressed models keep their accuracy while flipping a sizeable share
of individual outputs \cite{dutta2024accuracy}. Our winner-change analysis applies this
observation to selection, where a changed winner usually carries the same answer.

\paragraph{Quantized evaluators.} The closest work quantizes LLM-as-judge models:
\citet{patira2026quantizing} report that INT8 judges stay within a preregistered
equivalence margin of their full-precision agreement with humans, while NF4 flips a
quarter to a third of pairwise verdicts on open-ended chat. We study scalar reward
models used for Best-of-$N$ selection on problems with verifiable answers, several
quantization methods, a memory frontier with packed kernels, and selection value relative
to majority vote. To our knowledge, no prior work quantizes the verifier in Best-of-$N$
and measures the effect on selection.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice.} Math benchmark results are sensitive to answer
extraction, contamination and label errors \cite{zhang2024a,vendrow2025platinum,
hochlehnert2025a,biderman2024lessons}, and reporting confidence intervals is still
uncommon \cite{miller2024adding}. Preregistration has been proposed for predictive
modelling \cite{hofman2023preregistration} but remains rare in machine learning. We fix
the analysis plans before the data exist, report statistical uncertainty for every
contrast, and check the outcome measure with blinded human grading, revised labels and a
lenient grader.
