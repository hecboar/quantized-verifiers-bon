\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Verifiers and test-time compute} Training a verifier to rank sampled
solutions was introduced for grade-school mathematics by Cobbe et
al.~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}. Process supervision, which scores every reasoning step,
outperforms outcome supervision on MATH \cite{lightman2023verify}, and automatically
labelled PRMs \cite{wang2023mathshepherd,setlur2024rewarding,zhang2025prm} and
generative verifiers \cite{zhang2024generative} have followed. How to spend inference
compute between sampling and verification has become a research question in itself
\cite{snell2024scaling,liu2025can,singhi2025when}. These works study what verifiers can
achieve at full precision; the verifier's own cost enters only as a fixed term.

\paragraph{Reward models against self-consistency} Majority vote over sampled answers
(self-consistency) needs no verifier \cite{wang2023selfconsistency}, and its coverage
and accuracy keep growing with the number of samples
\cite{brown2024large,wu2024inference}. Whether a reward model beats it depends on the
generator, the reward model and the aggregation rule
\cite{yuan2024free,di2025bestofmajority}; calibrated combinations of answer frequency
and PRM scores can outperform both \cite{kuang2025aggregation}. In a study of
memory-optimal test-time strategies for reasoning models, Kim et
al.~\cite{kim2025not} include a Best-of-$N$ experiment with an external 7B PRM counted
at full precision (13.28\,GB; Section~4.1 of the ICLR 2026 version) and find it less
memory-efficient than majority voting. Their main analysis quantizes the generator and
excludes verifiers by design. Our study addresses the complementary question: how much
of a verifier's cost can be removed by quantizing the verifier itself, and whether its
selections survive.

\paragraph{Cheaper verification} Other routes reduce the cost of Best-of-$N$
verification without compressing the reward model: rejecting low-scoring generations
early \cite{sun2024speculative}, learning lightweight rewards from the generator's
hidden states \cite{guo2025swift}, or scoring from the generator's key--value cache
instead of re-encoding the text \cite{kuang2026kvprm}. These change what is computed;
weight quantization keeps the verifier and its inputs unchanged and only lowers the
precision of its weights, so it can be combined with them.

\paragraph{Reward-model reliability} Reward-model quality is usually measured on
preference and step-error benchmarks \cite{lambert2024rewardbench,zheng2024processbench,
song2025prmbench}, and Best-of-$N$ is known to over-optimise imperfect reward models
\cite{gao2023overoptimization}. Our question is different: not whether a reward model
is accurate, but whether a compressed copy of it selects as well as the original.

\paragraph{Post-training quantization of language models} Post-training quantization
reduces memory with little loss of perplexity or task accuracy at 8 bits
\cite{dettmers2022llmint,xiao2022smoothquant} and, for weights only, at 4 bits
\cite{frantar2023gptq,lin2024awq,dettmers2023qlora}; 4-bit precision is close to optimal
for a fixed bit budget \cite{dettmers2022the}, and large evaluations find that
well-calibrated 4- and 8-bit models track their full-precision versions
\cite{kurtic2024give}. The topic is active in this journal as well
\cite{zhu2027rotsq,bang2027bvsr}. For reasoning, quantizing the \emph{generator} hurts
more at 3 bits and on harder problems \cite{liu2025quantization,li2025quantization}.
Aggregate accuracy can also hide many changed outputs: compressed models keep their
accuracy while flipping a sizeable share of individual answers
\cite{dutta2024accuracy}. We measure the analogue for selection: how often the selected
candidate, and how often its answer, changes.

\paragraph{Quantized evaluators} The closest work quantizes LLM-as-judge models: a
concurrent workshop submission \cite{anon2026judges} reports that INT8 judges keep
their agreement with human labels within a preregistered equivalence region on
pairwise chat comparisons, while NF4 flips a quarter to a third of verdicts and lowers
one judge's agreement. We study scalar reward models used for Best-of-$N$ selection on
problems with verifiable answers, several quantization methods, a memory comparison
with packed kernels, and the verifier's advantage over majority vote. We did not find
prior work that quantizes the verifier in Best-of-$N$ and measures the effect on
selection.

\paragraph{Evaluation practice} Math benchmark results are sensitive to answer
extraction, contamination and label errors \cite{zhang2024a,vendrow2025platinum,
hochlehnert2025a,biderman2024lessons}, and confidence intervals are still uncommon in
model evaluations \cite{miller2024adding}. Preregistration has been proposed for
predictive modelling \cite{hofman2023preregistration} but remains rare in machine
learning. We specify the analysis plans prospectively in version control, report
uncertainty for every contrast, and check the outcome measure with a lenient grader, a
human audit and revised labels.
