\subsection{Four-bit quantization preserves Best-of-32 accuracy}\label{sec:equivalence}

The registered contrast c1 (8B ORM, GPTQ-W4, original 1,200-problem manifest, BF16
accuracy 77.5\%) is $\Delta_{32} = +0.33$\pp\ with a 90\% interval of $[-0.58, +1.25]$,
inside the registered $\pm3$\pp\ margin ($p_{\mathrm{TOST}} \le 10^{-4}$, which passes
any Holm threshold of the family): \textbf{equivalence is supported}. On this population
a 3\pp\ loss would have meant 13\% more errors. On the full 2,000 problems the same
contrast is $+0.15$\pp\ $[-0.65, +0.95]$ (95\% interval; Table~\ref{tab:main}).

Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the exploratory grid on the 2,000 problems. At 4 bits,
GPTQ-W4 and NF4 change $A_{32}$ by between $-1.1$ and $+0.6$\pp\ for the three ORMs and
the PRM, while the selected candidate changes on 21--49\% of problems; the 4-bit curves
track BF16 at every budget $N\le32$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}). Three bits is where
selection degrades with this recipe: GPTQ-W3 costs 1.3--1.8\pp\ for the 4B, 8B and PRM
verifiers and 13.2\pp\ for the 1.7B ORM, whose selection falls towards the
random-candidate rate. The quantization method matters little at 4 bits for the 4B and
8B ORMs (AWQ-W4: $0.00$ and $+0.55$\pp; round-to-nearest RTN-W4: $+0.25$ and
$+0.90$\pp; RTN-W8 on the 8B: $+0.25$\pp), but the 1.7B ORM is sensitive to it: RTN-W4
costs it $2.8$\pp\ $[-4.0, -1.7]$ against $1.1$\pp\ for GPTQ-W4.

\subsection{Under the 6~GB verifier budget, the tested 4B 4-bit verifier is best}\label{sec:frontier}

Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} shows accuracy against measured verifier memory for the tested
Skywork ORM deployments. At the registered 6~GB ceiling, the configuration selected on
development data is the 4B ORM with packed GPTQ-W4 (4.9~GiB peak), and the best BF16
verifier that fits is the 1.7B ORM (4.0~GiB). (The plan states the ceiling as 6~GB and
it was applied to the GiB measurements; the selected configuration, 4.9~GiB = 5.3~GB,
fits either reading.) The registered contrast c2 is $+2.25$\pp\ $[+1.05, +3.45]$,
$p \le 10^{-4}$, Holm $p \le 0.0005$: \textbf{supported}. The 1.7B BF16 verifier is
0.3\pp\ below majority vote, which needs no verifier memory at all, whereas the 4B
GPTQ-W4 verifier is 2.0\pp\ above it. The comparison covers the tested checkpoints, a
fixed scoring workload and verifier memory only; within this family, accuracy does not
grow with size (the 4B BF16 model beats the 8B), so the result is about these
deployments rather than a general rule that larger is better.

Part of the advantage is answer format rather than answer content. With the lenient
grader, c2 is $+1.55$\pp\ $[+0.45, +2.65]$. As a secondary analysis, one of the authors
graded, blind to arm, verifier and grader verdict, every distinct Best-of-32 winner
without an extractable final answer in either arm (94 responses) and 30 random winners
with one (\ref{app:audit}); identical responses selected in both arms received a single
label. Replacing the strict grader by these labels on all audited winners gives
$+1.40$\pp\ $[+0.30, +2.50]$, nominal $p=0.011$ (4B GPTQ-W4 75.5\% versus 1.7B BF16
74.1\%); this interval treats the human labels as error-free and the analysis is not
part of the registered family. The registered strict-grader effect is therefore an
upper estimate, and we regard $+1.4$\pp\ as the more realistic size. It does not depend
on label noise or truncation: with GSM8K labels replaced by GSM8K-Platinum it is
$+2.37$\pp, and with length-truncated candidates removed before selection $+2.20$\pp\
(strict grader). \todo{memory/throughput re-measurement incl. PRM (exp. C)}

\subsection{Selected candidates change often; selected answers rarely do}\label{sec:mechanism}

Table~\ref{tab:transitions} decomposes the 4-bit and 3-bit changes into answer
transitions. With GPTQ-W4 or NF4 the selected candidate changes on 21--49\% of
main-study problems, but the selected canonical answer changes on only 4.5--12.8\%:
73--81\% of the candidate changes keep the same final answer. Harm (a correct BF16
selection becomes wrong) and benefit (the reverse) are each 0.9--4.1\% of problems and
largely offset each other. At 3 bits both the answer-change rate (12.6--37.8\%) and
harm grow, and harm exceeds benefit. The same pattern holds in H6 and H7. These
measurements are consistent with answer redundancy: candidates that share the selected
answer are interchangeable for accuracy, so most score perturbations move the selection
within the same answer cluster.

\subsection{No confirmatory evidence for the MATH-loss and budget-growth contrasts}\label{sec:c4c5}

Development data had suggested that quantization damage concentrates on MATH and grows
with $N$. On the 1,200 evaluation MATH problems, c4 (MATH $\Delta_{32}$, 4B and 8B ORMs
at GPTQ-W4, averaged within problem) is $-0.63$\pp\ $[-1.38, +0.08]$, Holm $p=0.40$; c5
(the change of that loss from $N=4$ to $N=32$) is $-0.51$\pp\ $[-1.20, +0.16]$, Holm
$p=0.42$. Neither is supported. These are inconclusive results, not evidence of no
effect; the intervals exclude MATH losses larger than about 1.4\pp. c4 is a within-MATH
contrast, not a test of an interaction with difficulty; the development signal came
from 200 problems and did not replicate.

\subsection{A stronger generator on hard problems (H6)}\label{sec:h6}

H6 asked whether the result survives a stronger generator on harder, previously unused
problems: Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct \cite{yang2024qwen25math}, 800 MATH Level-5 test
problems, 32 candidates with up to 2,048 tokens, the 8B ORM and the PRM in BF16 and
GPTQ-W4. Its family of four tests asked for equivalence at $\pm3$\pp\ (H6a) and for
retention of at least half of the BF16 verifier's advantage over majority vote (H6b).
With $G = A_{\mathrm{BF16}} - A_{\mathrm{MV}}$ the BF16 advantage and $\Delta$ the
quantization effect, H6b tests
\[
R \;=\; \tfrac12 G + \Delta \;=\; (A_{\mathrm{Q}} - A_{\mathrm{MV}}) - \tfrac12 G \;>\; 0,
\]
i.e.\ the quantized verifier's advantage exceeds half of the BF16 advantage. $R$ is
computed per problem, $R_i = C_{\mathrm{Q},i} - \tfrac12(C_{\mathrm{BF16},i} +
C_{\mathrm{MV},i})$, and bootstrapped as a mean. $R>0$ implies retained value only when
$G>0$; the plans therefore interpret H6b/H7b only for verifiers whose BF16 advantage is
positive, which we check descriptively with 97.5\% intervals.

Equivalence is supported for both verifiers: $\Delta_{32} = 0.00$\pp\ $[-1.13, +1.13]$
for the ORM and $+0.50$\pp\ $[-0.50, +1.50]$ for the PRM, although the selected
candidate changes on 31\% and 34\% of problems. With this generator, however, neither
verifier showed a clear positive advantage over majority vote (76.5\%): the PRM tied it
($G = +0.13$\pp\ $[-1.88, +2.13]$) and the ORM fell below it ($G = -3.75$\pp\ $[-6.25,
-1.25]$). H6 therefore did not establish a positive BF16 advantage to retain, which
limits the practical interpretation of its retention tests (ORM $R = -1.88$\pp, PRM
$+0.56$\pp; neither supported). These conclusions hold under the lenient grader and
without truncated candidates (19\% of candidates reached the length limit).

\subsection{Value retention where the verifier adds value (H7)}\label{sec:h7}

H7 is a prospective, targeted follow-up in a regime selected with earlier exploratory
data: the main generator on hard problems, where the main-study PRM and 8B ORM had beaten
majority vote. It uses all 1,060 MATH Level-4 and Level-5 test problems not used
anywhere else in this work, the registered generation settings, and the same verifiers,
conditions, tests and analysis code as H6. Because the regime was chosen with knowledge
of earlier results, H7 estimates the effect of quantization in that regime, not the
typical value of a verifier across deployments.

Both verifiers beat majority vote in BF16 on the new problems: the PRM by $+8.2$\pp\
(97.5\% interval $[+5.9, +10.7]$) and the 8B ORM by $+3.0$\pp\ $[+0.4, +5.8]$. All four
registered hypotheses are supported (Holm over four). Accuracy is equivalent at
$\pm3$\pp: $\Delta_{32} = -0.28$\pp\ $[-1.42, +0.85]$ for the ORM and $-0.57$\pp\
$[-1.32, +0.19]$ for the PRM. The quantized verifiers keep more than half of their BF16
advantage: decisively for the PRM ($R = +3.54$\pp\ $[+2.45, +4.72]$, Holm $p \le
0.0003$), narrowly for the ORM ($R = +1.23$\pp\ $[-0.14, +2.59]$, one-sided Holm
$p = 0.041$), in line with its lower planned power. As descriptive quantities, the PRM
retains 93\% of its advantage (95\% interval 84--102\%) and the ORM 91\% (44--149\%;
the ratio is unstable because the ORM's advantage is small). The retention conclusions
keep their sign under the lenient grader and without truncated candidates, the
registered condition for describing value as preserved. Three bits is not safe in this
regime (exploratory): GPTQ-W3 costs $3.5$\pp\ (ORM) and $2.6$\pp\ (PRM) and removes the
ORM's advantage. Level-wise estimates and all sensitivity analyses are in the
repository. Fig.~\ref{fig:retention} summarises the advantage over majority vote before
and after quantization across regimes.

\paragraph{Selection rules} The 4-bit result does not depend on the selection rule
(exploratory, main study, $N=32$): with a reward-weighted answer vote (temperature
fitted on development data), GPTQ-W4 changes accuracy by $-1.00$, $-0.60$, $+0.30$ and
$+0.25$\pp\ for the 1.7B, 4B and 8B ORMs and the PRM; restricting Best-of-32 to
candidates with an extractable answer gives $-1.10$, $-0.55$, $0.00$ and $-0.45$\pp; and
the PRM's last-step aggregation gives $-0.25$\pp. GPTQ-W3 again loses 0.7--11\pp\ under
every rule. Restricting selection to extractable answers raises the BF16 ORMs' accuracy
by 2.2--3.2\pp\ (e.g.\ 8B: 72.9\% to 76.1\%), a measure of how much the strict grader
penalises their preference for prose answers.

\paragraph{Answer format} Several quantized conditions appeared to \emph{improve} GSM8K
accuracy under the strict grader (e.g.\ 4B GPTQ-W4 $+3.0$\pp; $+8.0$\pp\ on the Llama
pools). A decomposition into format and content shows these are format effects:
quantization weakens the Skywork ORMs' preference for prose answers without a boxed
final answer (4B GPTQ-W4: format term $+3.0$, content term $0.0$; lenient $+0.4$\pp).
MATH effects are content effects under both graders. We report no quantization gain.

\paragraph{Packed versus simulated quantization} Packed and simulated GPTQ-W4 select the
same final answer on 98.8--99.2\% of problems; correctness flips are rare and symmetric
(0.35--0.55\% of problems), and packed GPTQ-W4 changes accuracy versus BF16 by
$-1.05$, $+0.55$ and $+0.20$\pp\ for the 1.7B, 4B and 8B ORMs (simulated: $-1.10$,
$+0.55$, $+0.15$). c2 uses the packed scores.

\paragraph{Second generator} On 300 problems with Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct candidates
(exploratory), GPTQ-W4 changes the PRM's Best-of-32 accuracy by $+0.33$\pp\ $[-3.00,
+4.00]$ and NF4 by $-0.67$\pp; for the 4B ORM the strict grader shows $+8.0$\pp, which
shrinks to $+2.0$\pp\ $[0.0, +4.3]$ under the lenient grader, again a format effect.
With this generator, majority vote (76.3\%) beats both verifiers in BF16 (ORM 63.7\%,
PRM 70.7\%).

\paragraph{Labels and truncation} With GSM8K-Platinum labels c1 is $+0.44$\pp\ (c4 and
c5 involve MATH only and are unchanged); with truncated candidates removed before
selection, c1 is $+0.33$, c4 $-0.67$ and c5 $-0.52$\pp. No conclusion changes. Both
GSM8K problems whose labels the human audit had flagged as questionable are among those
GSM8K-Platinum removes.
