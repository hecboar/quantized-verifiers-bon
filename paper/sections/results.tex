\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Four-bit verifiers select as well as BF16 verifiers}\label{sec:equivalence}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports Best-of-32 accuracy on the 2,000 evaluation problems.
The registered contrast c1 (8B ORM, GPTQ-W4, original 1,200-problem population) is
$\Delta_{32} = +0.33$\pp\ with a 90\% interval of $[-0.58, +1.25]$, inside the
registered $\pm3$\pp\ margin ($p_{\mathrm{TOST}} < 10^{-4}$): \textbf{equivalence is
supported}. A 3\pp\ loss would have meant 13\% more errors relative to BF16; the
interval also lies inside $\pm1.5$\pp\ (post hoc). The same holds across the
exploratory grid at 4 bits: for the three ORMs and the PRM, GPTQ-W4 and NF4 change
$A_{32}$ by between $-1.1$ and $+0.6$\pp, even though the selected candidate changes
on 21--49\% of problems. Across budgets $N\in\{1,\dots,32\}$ the 4-bit curves track
BF16 (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}).

\subsection{Under a memory budget, quantize the larger verifier}\label{sec:frontier}

Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} shows the accuracy--memory frontier of deployed verifiers.
At the registered 6~GB ceiling, the configuration selected on development data is the
4B ORM with packed GPTQ-W4 (4.9~GiB peak), against the best BF16 verifier that fits,
the 1.7B ORM (4.0~GiB). The registered contrast c2 is $+2.25$\pp\ $[+1.05, +3.45]$,
$p<10^{-4}$, Holm-adjusted $p = 0.0005$: \textbf{supported}.

About a third of this advantage is answer format rather than answer content. With the
lenient grader the effect is $+1.55$\pp\ $[+0.45, +2.65]$. To measure it directly, a
human rater graded, blind to arm, verifier and grader verdicts, every Best-of-32
winner without an extractable final answer in either arm (94 responses) plus 30
random controls; a blinded second pass resolved every ``unsure'' verdict
(Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). With these human verdicts replacing the strict grader on
all audited winners, c2 is $+1.40$\pp\ $[+0.30, +2.50]$ (4B GPTQ-W4 75.5\% versus 1.7B
BF16 74.1\%). We therefore report $+1.4$\pp\ as the format-corrected size of the
registered effect. It does not depend on label noise or truncation: with GSM8K
labels replaced by GSM8K-Platinum (56 ambiguous problems removed, 8 relabelled) it is
$+2.37$\pp, and with length-truncated candidates removed before selection $+2.20$\pp\
(both under the strict grader).

\subsection{Why selection is robust: answer redundancy}\label{sec:mechanism}

Quantization perturbs verifier scores enough to change the selected candidate on a
quarter to a half of problems at 4 bits, yet accuracy barely moves. The reason is
redundancy in the pool: on most problems many candidates share the selected answer,
so a different winner usually carries the same final answer. Harm concentrates on
\emph{contested} problems, where the BF16 winner's answer is held by 20--50\% of the
pool. On these problems (lenient grader), GPTQ-W3 costs the 1.7B ORM 31.0\pp\
$[-35.9, -25.9]$ ($n=410$) and the 4B and 8B ORMs 5.4 and 5.7\pp, while GPTQ-W4 costs
at most 3.4\pp\ (1.7B) and 0.0--1.2\pp\ for the larger models
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}). The BF16-null control shows the same direction of
movement at a much smaller scale, consistent with score noise tipping close
decisions. The redundancy bins were fixed after first inspection; this analysis is
exploratory.

\subsection{Difficulty and budget did not amplify the damage}\label{sec:c4c5}

Development data suggested that quantization damage concentrates on MATH and grows
with $N$; the registered tests on the 1,200 evaluation MATH problems do not support
this. c4 (MATH $\Delta_{32}$, 4B and 8B GPTQ-W4 averaged) is $-0.63$\pp\
$[-1.38, +0.08]$, Holm $p=0.40$; c5 (growth from $N=4$ to $N=32$) is $-0.51$\pp\
$[-1.20, +0.16]$, Holm $p=0.42$. Both are reported as not supported (inconclusive,
not evidence of absence); the intervals bound any such effect at about 1.4\pp. The
development signal was a winner's-curse estimate on 200 problems.

\subsection{A stronger generator on hard problems (H6)}\label{sec:h6}

To test whether robustness survives where selection is harder, we registered a
follow-up study before generating any data: Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct
\cite{yang2024qwen25math}, 800 MATH Level-5 test problems never used in this work,
32 candidates with up to 2,048 tokens, the 8B ORM and the PRM in BF16 and GPTQ-W4.
Its family of four tests asked for equivalence at $\pm3$\pp\ (H6a) and for retention
of at least half of the verifier's advantage over majority vote (H6b,
$R = \tfrac12\,\overline{\mathrm{gain}} + \overline{\Delta} > 0$).

Equivalence is supported for both verifiers: $\Delta_{32} = 0.00$\pp\ $[-1.13,
+1.13]$ for the ORM and $+0.50$\pp\ $[-0.50, +1.50]$ for the PRM (Holm
$p<10^{-4}$), although quantization changes the selected candidate on 31\% and 35\% of
problems. H6b could not succeed: with this generator, majority vote (76.5\%) matched
the PRM (76.6\%) and beat the ORM (72.8\%), so there was no advantage to retain
(ORM $R=-1.88$\pp, PRM $+0.56$\pp; not supported). The same holds with the lenient
grader and without truncated candidates (19\% of candidates reached the limit).
Quantization-induced changes moved selections towards the consensus answer: on
problems where the BF16 PRM had picked an answer different from majority vote
($n=131$), its quantized version was right 6.9\pp\ more often $[+3.1, +11.5]$
(exploratory).

\subsection{Robustness checks}\label{sec:robustness}

\paragraph{Answer format.} Several quantized conditions appeared to \emph{improve}
GSM8K accuracy (e.g.\ 4B GPTQ-W4 $+3.0$\pp; $+8.0$\pp\ on the Llama pools). A
decomposition into format and content shows these are format effects: in BF16 the
Skywork ORMs slightly prefer prose answers without a boxed final answer, which the
strict grader scores as wrong, and quantization weakens that preference (4B GPTQ-W4:
format term $+3.0$, content term $0.0$; lenient $+0.4$\pp). MATH effects are content
effects under both graders. We do not report any quantization ``gain''.
